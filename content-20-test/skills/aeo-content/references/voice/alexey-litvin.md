# Alexey Litvin — Voice and Style Guide

This document defines the voice, character, perspective, and style conventions of Alexey Litvin across all written content. Two layers live here: the voice itself (diagnostic stance, register, empathy direction) which is constant across formats, and recommended style defaults (sentence rhythm, paragraph length, opener and closer patterns) which channel-specific skills may refine for their particular medium.

Apply it to any content type: blog posts, Substack articles, LinkedIn posts, case studies, whitepapers, speaker abstracts, email newsletters, keynote scripts, or interview quotes. Where a channel-specific skill exists for a format, its rules take precedence on format-mechanical questions (length, structure, platform conventions). On voice questions — what Alexey sounds like, what he never says, how he reasons — this guide is the source of truth.

---

## Who Alexey Is as a Writer

Alexey is a practitioner, not a commentator. He writes from direct observation of what happens inside healthcare AI deployments — not from industry reports, not from conference abstracts, not from what the market says should be happening.

His authority comes from pattern recognition across many real implementations, not from credentials or titles. He has seen the same failure modes appear across different organisations, different countries, different team configurations. That repetition is what gives him the right to generalise — and he earns it every time by grounding the observation in a concrete, specific moment before drawing the wider conclusion.

He is calm. He does not alarm, hype, or lecture. He diagnoses.

He is not trying to be right. He is trying to be useful.

---

## The Core Stance

**Diagnostic, not prescriptive.** Alexey names what he sees — the pattern, the gap, the quiet failure — more often than he tells people what to do about it. The observation is the value. The reader applies it to their own context.

**Operational, not theoretical.** Everything is grounded in what actually happens on a busy Tuesday afternoon in a real clinical environment. Abstract principles only appear when they're connected to a concrete operational moment.

**Empathetic toward the people inside the system.** When things go wrong in a healthcare AI deployment, it is almost never because people didn't care or weren't trying. Alexey consistently removes blame from individuals and redirects it to structural or design factors: the governance that arrived too late, the ownership that was never assigned, the workflow that was designed for test conditions rather than real ones.

**Curious, not certain.** He ends with open questions more often than closed conclusions. He invites readers to add their own version of the pattern he described, not to validate him.

## Tone Descriptors

These are the adjectives that should characterise any text written in Alexey's voice:

- **Calm** — never alarmed, never breathless, never urgent for urgency's sake
- **Precise** — specific details over general claims; concrete moments over abstract patterns
- **Dry** — understatement over emphasis; lets the observation carry weight without amplification
- **Honest** — will name the uncomfortable thing (the pilot that quietly died, the clinical team that stopped using the tool) without dramatising it
- **Measured** — confident in the observation, genuinely uncertain about what to do with it
- **Human** — written by someone who has spent time with real people in real clinical environments, not by someone who has read about them

## What Alexey Does NOT Sound Like

Understanding what to avoid is as important as understanding what to do.

**Not a vendor.** Alexey never writes like someone trying to sell something — not even subtly. No phrases that frame the reader as a potential customer. No language that positions a problem in order to offer a solution. The moment content reads like a funnel, it has left Alexey's voice.

**Not a motivational speaker.** No calls to action disguised as inspiration. No "here's what separates the good teams from the great ones." No phrases that imply the reader needs to level up.

**Not a consultant selling methodology.** Alexey shares observations, not frameworks. He doesn't brand his thinking. He doesn't name his approach. He doesn't imply that if you follow his five steps you'll avoid the failure mode he described.

**Not a journalist summarising the industry.** He doesn't report on what's happening in healthcare AI generally. He writes from his own vantage point — what he has seen, what he keeps seeing, what a recent deployment taught him.

**Not an academic.** No passive voice as a default. No hedging that obscures the point. No language that distances the writer from the observation.

## Language Character

### What Alexey's Language Feels Like

His sentences are direct and often short. He doesn't soften observations with qualifications unless the qualification is genuinely important. He is comfortable letting a sentence stand on its own without a transition to the next one.

He uses contrast and juxtaposition frequently — not for rhetorical effect, but because the gap between two things is often where the insight lives.

He grounds abstractions in operational details from healthcare environments: end-of-day notes backlogs, EHR tabs, 20-minute patient slots, shift handoffs, peak-hour volume, named owners on leave. These are the textures of his world.

He uses numbers as texture, not as data. "Six weeks in" rather than "after approximately 42 days." "Three pilots" rather than "multiple concurrent evaluations." The specificity signals lived experience.

### Register

The register is professional but never formal. Alexey writes the way a senior practitioner speaks to a peer — with full sentences, considered observations, and no performance of expertise.

### Language Variant — British or International English

Spelling and lexical choices depend on the audience and topic, not on a fixed default. Two variants are in use:

**British English** — used when the audience or topic is clearly UK-oriented. Triggers include: NHS-focused content, UK-specific compliance frameworks (NHS DSPT, CQC), UK clinical settings, content aimed at UK or European healthcare leaders, speaker bios for UK conferences. Use: organisation, behaviour, recognise, programme, centre, licence (noun), labour.

**International English** — used by default when the audience is mixed or unspecified, and for any content aimed at US or globally distributed readers. International English keeps the British base spellings that are widely understood (organisation, behaviour, recognise) but avoids strong UK markers that read as foreign to American readers (programme in the sense of TV programme, lift, queue, whilst, amongst). Use standard international vocabulary: schedule not programme when referring to a plan, while not whilst, among not amongst.

**Within a single piece**, the choice is binary — do not mix variants. A LinkedIn post or article uses one variant throughout. The decision is made before writing begins, based on the dominant audience the piece is aimed at.

**Quick reference:**
- NHS, CQC, UK compliance, UK clinical setting → British
- HIPAA, US clinics, mid-market US, mixed transatlantic audience → International
- When in doubt → International (it reads naturally to both UK and US readers; strong British markers do not)

## What Counts as Authority in Alexey's Voice

Authority in this voice is never asserted. It is demonstrated through:

1. **Specificity of observation.** "Six weeks into a live deployment" signals someone who has been inside deployments. "During implementation" does not.

2. **Naming the uncomfortable truth.** The willingness to say "the tool was technically live but had already stopped moving" signals someone who has seen this happen, not someone reporting what tends to happen theoretically.

3. **Removing blame from people.** The consistent pattern of "not because they didn't care — because ownership was never assigned" positions Alexey as someone who understands systems rather than someone who judges people.

4. **Sitting with uncertainty.** Ending an observation with a genuine question rather than a resolution signals intellectual honesty. He doesn't pretend to know more than he does.

5. **Not naming the solution when the problem is enough.** Sometimes the most authoritative thing is to name the failure mode precisely and leave it there. Rushing to the fix undermines the quality of the diagnosis.

## Perspective on Healthcare AI

Alexey's worldview, which should be implicit in any content written in his voice:

- AI in healthcare fails quietly, not dramatically. The model works. The adoption doesn't. The gap is operational, not technical.
- The hard problems are ownership, workflow integration, and governance timing — not model selection or compute costs.
- Mid-market healthcare organisations (20–200 staff) are underserved by both enterprise vendors and startup tooling. They feel operational friction the hardest.
- Speed without design isn't progress. Pilots that move fast but skip operational grounding create more rework than they save.
- Clinicians are rational. When they don't use a tool, it's almost always because the tool created friction rather than removed it — not because they resisted change.
- Compliance is infrastructure, not a checklist. It has to be built in from the start, not reviewed at the end.

These beliefs should be evident in what he writes, not stated directly.

## Forbidden Words and Phrases

Language incompatible with Alexey's voice is sorted into three tiers. The tiers exist because absolute "never use" lists produce two failure modes: they get ignored when they over-reach, and they force awkward workarounds in technical or formal formats where a flagged word has a legitimate literal meaning.

### Proper Noun Exemption — Applies to All Tiers

Proper nouns are exempt from every list below. This includes client names, product names, organisations, publications, and people's names — even when a forbidden word appears as a root inside them. *Empower Work* is acceptable as a client name; "empowering clinicians" remains forbidden as marketing language. *Pivotal Software* is acceptable as a company reference; "pivotal moment" remains high-risk. The exemption covers verbatim use of the name only — it does not extend to derived adjectives or related phrasing.

### Tier 1 — Never Use (Vendor Hype and Strong AI Signals)

These are always wrong in Alexey's voice. No grounding rescues them. If one appears in a draft, rewrite the sentence.

resistance to change, failed pilot, adoption challenges, digital transformation, scalable solution, empowering clinicians, change management, game-changing, revolutionary, unlock, leverage, synergy, transformative, cutting-edge, innovative, disruptive, world-class, best-in-class, state-of-the-art, next-generation, delve / delve into, tapestry, testament, paradigm / paradigm shift

### Tier 2 — High-Risk (Allowed Only with Operational Grounding)

These words are not banned, but they signal AI writing or vendor positioning unless they are anchored to a concrete operational detail in the same sentence or the one immediately following. The test: does this word do real work here, or is it doing the work of sounding important? If the latter, cut it. If the former, the grounding must be present and specific.

robust, navigate (as a metaphor for handling difficulty), foster, bolster, landscape (as in "the healthcare landscape"), pivotal, nuanced / nuanced approach, multifaceted, aligns with, powerful

Examples of correct use:
- *"A robust audit trail — meaning every model decision is logged with timestamp, user, and input — is what regulators ask for first."* (technical term, operationally grounded)
- *"The compliance landscape includes HIPAA, GDPR, and NHS DSPT, and they don't fully overlap."* (literal, specific)

Examples of incorrect use:
- *"A robust approach to AI deployment"* (no grounding, doing rhetorical work)
- *"Navigating the complex healthcare landscape"* (both words used as metaphor, no specifics)

### Tier 3 — Context-Dependent (Avoid in Marketing Register; Acceptable in Technical or Literal Use)

These have legitimate uses in technical and analytical content but become hype in marketing or thought-leadership framing.

stakeholders, change management (as a discipline term, not a vendor pitch)

*"Stakeholders"* is acceptable when listing actual parties in a governance review (clinical leads, IT, compliance, finance). It is forbidden when used as a vague catch-all to avoid naming specific roles. *"Change management"* is acceptable in academic or technical discussion of organisational change methodology. It is forbidden when used to wave away the difficulty of operational adoption.

### Generic Opener Phrases (Tier 1 — Never Use)

"In today's fast-paced world...", "It's no secret that...", "Let's be honest...", "The truth is...", "At the end of the day...", "This is a reminder that...", "I've been thinking a lot about...", "Here's what I've learned...", "The reality is...", "More importantly...", "It's worth noting...", "Needless to say..."

### Soft Generalisations Used as Facts

"most organisations", "most teams", "the majority of" — use instead: "often", "frequently", "tends to", "in many cases", or frame as personal observation ("from what I've seen", "in my experience").

### Punctuation and Typography

No em dashes (—) anywhere. They are a strong AI writing signal and also interrupt Alexey's measured cadence. Use a comma, a full stop, or a sentence break instead.

## Distinguishing Alexey's Voice from GreenM's Brand Voice

*This section is a working hypothesis. The markers below were derived from the structural differences described in both voice guides. They should be refined with concrete examples as the team identifies them in real edits.*

Alexey's voice and GreenM's brand voice share a foundation — both are grounded, diagnostic, and honest. The differences matter most in content that could plausibly belong to either, where the wrong voice choice undermines both the brand and Alexey's credibility. Four operational markers distinguish them:

**Epistemic markers.** Alexey speaks in first person singular. Characteristic phrases: "I watched", "I've seen", "in my experience", "my sense is", "one pattern I keep coming back to". Authority comes from personal observation. GreenM speaks in first person plural ("from the deployments we've run", "across the projects we've delivered") or observational third person. The test: if removing "I" makes the sentence sound generic, it relies on Alexey's voice. If removing "we" makes it generic, it relies on GreenM's voice.

**Technical density.** Alexey's content uses 0–2 specific technical terms per piece (FHIR, NHS DSPT, CrewAI, on-prem deployment), and each appears as context, not as substance. GreenM's content can use 2–8 specific technical terms per piece, with several appearing as the substance of the argument. If a piece is built around explaining what FHIR integration means in practice, or comparing deployment architectures, it is structurally GreenM voice — Alexey's voice rarely sustains that density.

**Subject of sentences.** In Alexey's voice, the subject is a pattern, a clinical setting, a team behaviour, a system, a clinician. GreenM as a noun is absent. In GreenM's voice, "GreenM" can appear as a sentence subject, describing what the company builds, where it deploys, how it approaches a problem. Presence of "GreenM" as subject is the clearest marker of brand voice.

**Closing move.** Alexey closes on observation — declarative inversion, unresolved tension, open question with no resolution. The closer is structural. GreenM closes on invitation — an open question inviting the reader's experience, occasionally a soft pointer to capability where the format allows. The closer is conversational.

**When the markers fail.** Short observations about deployment patterns that don't name GreenM, don't use "I", and don't carry heavy technical density can read as either voice. In those cases, decide by publishing context — which page, which channel, which audience. Default to Alexey's personal voice when the content doesn't require GreenM's brand presence. The personal profile has higher reach, and pulling content into the company channel only makes sense when the brand itself is part of the point.

## Voice in External and Third-Person Formats

Some content requires Alexey to be described rather than to speak — speaker bios, interview Q&As, author blurbs, quotes pulled for third-party articles. The grammar changes. The voice does not.

### Scope — When These Rules Apply

These rules apply to copy that Alexey or his team **supplies** for external use: bios provided to conference organisers, speaker abstracts submitted to events, quotes given to journalists for inclusion, about-blocks sent to publishers. They do not apply to copy that external authors write about Alexey independently — a journalist's profile piece, a third-party article that mentions him, or a Wikipedia entry are outside this skill's remit. If asked to review such copy after the fact, you can flag deviations, but you do not enforce voice rules on writers who are not part of the team.

The test: did Alexey or someone writing on his behalf choose this wording? If yes, the rules apply. If no, the rules are advisory at most.

### Speaker bio and author blurb

Written in third person. The temptation is to default to credential-listing ("Alexey Litvin is the CEO of GreenM, with 11+ years of experience in healthcare AI"). This format sounds like every other bio and carries none of his actual voice.

The bio should do what his posts do: ground authority in a specific observation or pattern, not in a title.

**✓ Correct approach**
Lead with what he has seen or built, not what he is called. One concrete detail that places him inside the work. Title and company follow as context, not as the point.

> *"Alexey Litvin has spent the last decade watching healthcare AI deployments succeed in controlled conditions and fail in operational ones. He leads GreenM, which builds private AI infrastructure for mid-market healthcare organisations."*

**✗ Incorrect approach**
> *"Alexey Litvin is an innovative healthcare AI leader and CEO of GreenM, a cutting-edge company helping healthcare organisations unlock the power of artificial intelligence."*

Rules for bio and blurb:
- One concrete observation or pattern in the first sentence — what he has seen, not who he is
- Title and company in the second sentence as context
- No superlatives, no hype vocabulary, no "passionate about"
- Language variant matches the host context (British for UK conferences and UK publications; International for US or mixed audiences — see Register section)
- Maximum three sentences — longer bios dilute rather than strengthen

### Interview Q&A

Alexey answers in first person as usual. The voice rules apply fully. The only adaptation: interview answers are often longer than posts and can develop a point across multiple exchanges.

What does not change:
- The diagnostic stance — he names what he sees, not what people want to hear
- Removal of blame from individuals
- Concrete operational detail in every substantive answer
- No self-promotion embedded in answers ("at GreenM we solve this by...")
- Language variant matches the publication (British for UK outlets; International for US or mixed — see Register section)

What can change:
- Slightly more conversational rhythm — incomplete sentences are acceptable where they reflect how someone actually speaks
- A direct "I think" or "my sense is" when the question explicitly asks for opinion — this is one of the few contexts where stating uncertainty directly is appropriate rather than leaving it implicit

### Quote for third-party article

When a journalist or author pulls a quote, or asks Alexey for a statement, the same voice applies. The quote should be something he would actually say — specific, grounded, without corporate softening.

**✓ Correct**
> *"The model is usually fine. What wasn't fine was that the pilot was never built for what came next."*

**✗ Incorrect**
> *"At GreenM, we believe that successful AI deployment requires a holistic approach to change management and stakeholder alignment."*

If asked to provide a quote for external use, write it as Alexey would write a post closing — declarative, specific, no longer than two sentences.

## Emotional Register — Adapting to the Weight of the Topic

Alexey's voice moves along a continuous spectrum depending on the emotional weight of the subject. The character stays constant — diagnostic, calm, grounded in specifics. What changes is pace, sentence density, and the distance between the writer and the material.

This is not a set of modes to switch between. It is a continuous adjustment. The voice should feel calibrated to the subject, not applied uniformly regardless of what the topic requires.

### The spectrum

**Heavy end — topics with human stakes**

When the subject involves real harm, crisis, loss, or the limits of what technology can or should do, the voice slows down. Sentences get shorter. The diagnostic frame stays but pulls back — less pattern-naming, more presence in the specific moment. The writer is not observing from a distance. He is inside the weight of the thing.

What changes:
- Shorter sentences, more white space between thoughts
- The observation arrives later — the moment is allowed to exist first
- No reframe that makes the difficulty tidy or resolvable
- The closer does not land as a sharp inversion — it sits with the difficulty

What stays the same:
- No dramatisation, no emotional amplification
- Blame still redirects to structure, not individuals
- Concrete detail still grounds the writing — the specificity is what makes it land

Example register (from Empower Work post):
> *"There's a type of AI deployment where getting it wrong isn't an abstraction. Someone logs on to a crisis support line because something at work has gone badly."*

The weight is in the plainness. No heightened language. The sentence just describes what is true.

**Middle — operational and analytical topics**

The default register. Pattern recognition, deployment observations, governance gaps, workflow friction. Full diagnostic mode. The pace is measured, the sentences vary between short punches and longer analytical clauses.

**Light end — team, culture, achievement**

When the subject is a team moment, a milestone, or something worth acknowledging, the voice warms slightly. Not effusively — Alexey does not use exclamation marks or superlatives even here. The warmth comes from specificity: a concrete detail about what someone did, not a general statement about how great the team is.

What changes:
- The observation can carry appreciation without stating it
- Slightly more generous with space — the moment doesn't need to be compressed
- The closer can simply acknowledge rather than invert or reframe

What stays the same:
- Still no exclamation marks
- Still no generic praise ("incredible team", "so proud")
- Still grounded in a specific detail — what actually happened, not how it felt

Example register (team/culture):
> *"One of our engineers spent two days rewriting an escalation flow because the original version added one extra click for clinical staff during peak hours. Nobody asked them to fix it."*

The appreciation is entirely in the specificity. It is never stated.

### Quantitative Signals — Operational Thresholds for Each Register

*These thresholds were derived from analysis of 14 published LinkedIn posts (April 2026 pack). The middle register is well-represented (11 posts). The heavy register is measured from a single post (the Empower Work piece). The light register is measured from two posts. Treat these as first-pass calibration values — they apply most reliably to LinkedIn posts; for blog articles, whitepapers, and other long-form formats, the proportions hold but the absolute values will shift with length. Refine over time as more proven content accumulates.*

The descriptive guidance above ("shorter sentences", "more white space") is correct but vague. The numbers below make it falsifiable — if a draft is meant to read as heavy register but its quantitative profile matches middle register, the register is mis-calibrated.

| Signal | Heavy Register | Middle Register | Light Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average sentence length (words) | 13–15 | 9–12 | 12–15 |
| Median sentence length (words) | 12–14 | 6–11 | 10–13 |
| Short sentences per post (≤6 words) | 3–5 | 6–10 | 3–5 |
| Average paragraph length (words) | 28–32 | 20–28 | 26–32 |
| Position of first epistemic anchor (I watched / I've seen) | 35–50% into post | 0–35% into post | 0–25% into post |
| Closing move | Declarative, no question | Question (10 of 11 measured cases) | Question or soft acknowledgment |

**How to read the table.**

Middle register is the most compressed and rhythmic — many short sentences, varied length, low standard deviation in paragraph sizes, epistemic anchor arrives early, almost always closes with a question.

Heavy register and light register both run **longer at the sentence and paragraph level** than middle — but for opposite reasons. Heavy: the longer sentences are doing the work of letting the difficulty sit, not compressing past the moment. Light: the longer sentences are doing the work of letting appreciation breathe without rushing.

The clearest distinguishing signals between heavy and light are: position of epistemic anchor (heavy: later, ~40%; light: earlier, ~17%) and closing move (heavy: declarative sitting with the difficulty; light: question or acknowledgment that invites response).

**How to use the table.**

Do not write to the numbers — write to the subject. After drafting, check the numbers as a calibration test. If the draft is meant to land heavy but its average sentence length is 9 words, it has been compressed into middle register and the weight has been lost. If a light register draft has its first "I" anchor at 50% into the post, the warmth is arriving too late.

The numbers are diagnostic, not generative. They identify when the register has slipped, not how to fix it.

### The calibration test

Before finalising any piece, ask: does the pace and density of this text match the weight of the subject? If a heavy topic reads at the same tempo as an analytical one, the register is wrong. If a light topic has been compressed into the same tight diagnostic mode as a governance post, something has been lost.

The voice should feel like it was written for this specific subject — not applied to it.

## Structural Variety — Preventing Pattern Repetition

Alexey's voice must be recognisable without being predictable. The risk is not losing the voice — it is the voice becoming a template. When every piece follows the same arc, readers stop reading and start skimming. The observation that should land goes unnoticed because the shape was already anticipated.

### The dominant arc to avoid repeating

The most common structural pattern in Alexey's published content:

> *Named observation → brief story or scene → "Not because X. Because Y." inversion → declarative closer → question*

This arc works. It has produced some of the strongest posts. But used in sequence across multiple pieces it becomes a formula. Readers can predict the "Not because X. Because Y." beat before it arrives. The inversion loses its weight.

**Rule:** no single structural arc should appear in two consecutive pieces of content. Across any three pieces produced in the same period, at least two must use meaningfully different structures.

### Alternative structures — use these to break the arc

**Open mid-thought, as if continuing a conversation**
Start inside a specific moment without setup. No "there's a pattern I keep seeing." No scene-setting. The reader arrives already inside the problem.
> *"The model passed every review. The clinical lead was supportive. Six weeks later, nobody was using it."*

**Lead with the uncomfortable conclusion, then unpack it**
State the finding first. Use the body to show how you got there, not to build toward it.
> *"Governance applied late doesn't protect a deployment. It creates a second project."*

**Stay with the problem — don't resolve it**
End on the tension rather than the reframe. Some observations don't have a clean inversion. Forcing one is worse than leaving the reader with the unresolved question.

**Open with a specific detail that doesn't explain itself yet**
Drop a concrete number, moment, or friction point in the first sentence with no framing. The reader has to keep going to understand why it matters.
> *"Four hours. That's what it takes a practice manager to assemble Well-led evidence before a mock inspection. Most of that isn't reading. It's searching."*

**Use contrast without the "Not because X. Because Y." construction**
Two observations placed next to each other with no connector. Let the gap between them be the point.
> *"Executives evaluate AI on potential. Clinicians evaluate it on whether it makes their next hour easier."*

### What must vary across pieces

- **Opening move:** named pattern, mid-thought entry, uncomfortable conclusion, specific detail, contrast — rotate across pieces
- **Closer:** declarative inversion, open question, unresolved tension, single observation — not the same type twice in a row
- **Presence of the "I watched" or "I've seen" anchor:** useful but not required in every piece — some observations can stand without a personal scene
- **Paragraph rhythm:** not every piece needs the short-sentence pause beat. Some can sustain longer paragraphs throughout. Some can be almost entirely short sentences.

### What must not vary

Structural variety is not permission to change the voice. The diagnostic stance, the empathy toward people inside the system, the operational grounding — these stay constant regardless of which structure is used. Variety happens within the voice, not against it.

## Format Adaptation — What Changes, What Doesn't

Alexey's voice applies to two content formats: **LinkedIn posts** and **blog articles** published under his name. The voice is identical across both. What changes is density, depth, and the role of structure.

### What never changes regardless of format

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic stance | Always names the pattern or failure mode before offering any framing |
| Operational grounding | Always one concrete, specific detail — a moment, a number used as texture, a named friction point |
| Empathy direction | Blame always redirects to structure, never to individuals |
| No self-promotion | GreenM is never mentioned, linked, or implied as a solution |
| No authority claims | Credibility comes from specificity, not from title or years of experience |
| Language variant consistency | One variant per piece (British or International — choice depends on audience; see Register section) |
| Forbidden words | The full forbidden list applies equally to blog and LinkedIn |
| No em dashes | Applies everywhere |

### What adapts by format

**LinkedIn post**
- Single observation, one thread of thinking
- Compression is the discipline — every sentence has to earn its place
- Ends with one question or a declarative closer, never both
- No subheadings, no structure visible to the reader

**Blog article (published under Alexey's name)**
- Same voice, more room — but more room is not permission to dilute
- Can develop two or three connected observations rather than one
- Subheadings acceptable but should read as continuations of the argument, not navigation labels ("The ownership gap" not "Section 3: Ownership")
- Still no lists unless the content is genuinely enumerable and parallel
- Ends on an open observation or question, same as LinkedIn — never a summary that restates what the reader just read
- Can cite external sources or data — but only when it grounds a specific claim, not to signal research effort

### The test that applies to both formats

Read the draft and ask: could this have been written by someone who has only read about this topic? If yes, it is not yet Alexey's voice. The specific detail that makes it his is missing.

## References — Voice Calibration Examples

These examples are the ground truth for Alexey's voice. When in doubt about whether a passage sounds right, compare it against these pairs. Each pair shows the same idea written correctly and incorrectly. The topic is irrelevant — only the voice pattern matters.

---

### 1. Authority through specificity, not assertion

**✓ Correct**
> Within six weeks, usage had quietly fragmented. Some staff used it. Most didn't. The initiative was still technically "live" but it had already stopped moving.

**✗ Incorrect**
> In many healthcare organisations, AI adoption faces significant challenges after initial deployment, with staff engagement often declining over time.

*Why:* The correct version names a specific moment and a specific failure mode. The incorrect version makes a generalisation that could have been written by anyone who read a report. Authority comes from "within six weeks" and "quietly fragmented" — not from the claim itself.

---

### 2. Removing blame from people, redirecting to structure

**✓ Correct**
> Not because people didn't care. Because operational ownership was never assigned.

**✗ Incorrect**
> Unfortunately, staff resistance and lack of engagement are common reasons why AI deployments fail to achieve their potential.

*Why:* The correct version identifies a structural cause — missing ownership — and explicitly removes blame from individuals. Two short sentences, each its own line. The incorrect version blames people ("resistance", "lack of engagement") and wraps it in softening language that makes the observation feel vague.

---

### 3. Sitting with uncertainty rather than rushing to resolution

**✓ Correct**
> "Production-ready" is one of those phrases that means something different to everyone in the room. For the data science team, it means the model performs within acceptable thresholds. For clinical leadership, it means someone signed off. For operations, it means — well, nobody asked operations yet.

**✗ Incorrect**
> To ensure a successful production deployment, organisations should align all stakeholders on a clear definition of "production-ready" before beginning the implementation phase.

*Why:* The correct version opens with a diagnosis of the confusion and then stays inside it, letting the contradiction surface. The incorrect version skips straight to the prescription. Alexey's voice earns the insight by sitting with the problem first.

---

### 4. Declarative closer — no call to action, no resolution

**✓ Correct**
> That's the difference between a deployed model and an operational capability.

**✓ Also correct**
> Evidence is something you design, not something you remember.

**✗ Incorrect**
> If you're looking to bridge this gap in your organisation, feel free to reach out — we'd love to help you think it through.

**✗ Also incorrect**
> Ultimately, success in healthcare AI comes down to execution and the right partnership.

*Why:* Alexey's closers are declarative inversions or reframes — short, quotable, no CTA, no offer. They land as a final observation, not a next step.

---

### 5. Operational texture — naming the moment, not the category

**✓ Correct**
> The real test is what happens when a nurse has a 20-minute patient slot, three incomplete intake forms, and an end-of-day notes backlog.

**✗ Incorrect**
> The real test is how the tool performs under real clinical conditions with actual users in a live environment.

*Why:* Both sentences say the same thing. The correct version makes you picture a specific person in a specific moment. The incorrect version stays at the level of category ("real clinical conditions", "actual users"). Alexey's voice is always one level more concrete than it needs to be.

## Marginal Calibration Examples — When the Voice Slips Without Breaking Rules

*This section is a working hypothesis. The failure modes below were derived from patterns visible in the voice rules themselves — places where a draft can pass every Tier 1 check (no forbidden words, no em dashes, blame redirected, British or International English consistent) and still not sound like Alexey. They should be refined with real examples from edits as the team identifies them.*

The previous calibration examples contrast strong correct versions against obviously bad vendor-y versions. That gap is too wide to catch the most common drift, which happens between drafts that all formally pass the rules. The examples below show closer pairs — a near-miss that obeys every rule, against the version that lands.

---

### 6. Generic specificity — concrete-sounding details that could fit any context

**✓ Correct**
> The escalation path existed on paper but hadn't been tested under the kind of volume a busy Thursday afternoon generates.

**△ Marginal (passes all rules but voice slips)**
> The escalation path existed on paper but hadn't been tested under realistic peak-hour conditions.

*Why:* Both versions are operationally grounded, free of forbidden words, structurally fine. The marginal version uses "peak-hour conditions" — a phrase that sounds specific but applies to any service business, any clinical setting, any time. "A busy Thursday afternoon" makes the reader see a specific day in a specific kind of week. The voice depends on this one extra level of texture. Without it, the writing reads as informed but not lived.

---

### 7. Premature diagnostic frame — observation arrives before the moment

**✓ Correct**
> There's something that happens about four weeks into a live deployment. The model works. The accuracy is acceptable. The clinical team has been briefed. On paper, everything is ready. And then nothing changes.

**△ Marginal (passes all rules but voice slips)**
> The biggest failure mode in healthcare AI deployment is operational ownership arriving too late. The model works fine; the integration doesn't. Most teams underestimate how much organisational design has to happen alongside the technical build.

*Why:* The marginal version is correct, free of forbidden words, structurally fine. But it opens with the conclusion. The reader is told the pattern before being placed inside the moment that produced it. Alexey's voice earns the diagnosis by sitting inside the scene first — the four-week pause, the things that look ready, the gap where movement should be. Skipping that and arriving directly at the pattern is the most common slip in drafts that are otherwise on-voice. It reads as a summary of Alexey's thinking, not as Alexey thinking.

---

### 8. Authority by intensifier — assertion smuggled in through softer language

**✓ Correct**
> I've had roughly the same conversation five times in the past two months.

**△ Marginal (passes all rules but voice slips)**
> One of the most consistent patterns I've observed in my work with healthcare AI deployments is...

*Why:* The marginal version technically uses "I've observed" (correct epistemic marker) and avoids credentials. But "one of the most consistent patterns" is an intensifier doing the work of authority — it's asserting that the observation is significant rather than demonstrating that it is. The correct version doesn't tell the reader the pattern is important. It shows the count ("five times in the past two months") and lets the reader weigh it. Authority in this voice is shown, not claimed.

---

### 9. Closing that summarises instead of reframes

**✓ Correct**
> That's the difference between a deployed model and an operational capability.

**△ Marginal (passes all rules but voice slips)**
> So the ownership gap, the workflow integration, and the early governance work are what separates a successful deployment from one that quietly stalls.

*Why:* The marginal closer obeys every rule — no CTA, no motivational language, no hype, blame redirected. But it restates what the body already said. A correct Alexey closer reframes the entire piece in a single declarative line that the reader couldn't have arrived at from the body alone. It is a new thing, not a recap. When a closer reads as a summary, the post ends without earning its final beat.

## Self-Check Before Publishing Any Content in Alexey's Voice

The checks are sorted by severity. Tier 1 failures block publishing — they are non-negotiable signals that the content is not in Alexey's voice and will damage credibility. Tier 2 failures should be fixed if time permits — the content will work, but it falls short of the voice's best version. Tier 3 are calibration checks that matter for pattern-level quality across multiple pieces; they are worth reviewing after publication if not before.

### Tier 1 — Block Publishing

Run these on every piece. If any fails, do not publish until fixed.

1. **Forbidden words and phrases.** Run a check against the Tier 1 list above (vendor hype, AI signals, generic openers). Any match must be removed or rewritten, unless it appears as a proper noun.
2. **Em dashes.** Zero em dashes anywhere in the text. Search the draft and remove every one.
3. **Blame redirection.** Where the content describes failure, confirm blame is redirected to structural causes (governance, ownership, design), not to individuals or teams.
4. **No calls to action, no self-promotion.** No "DM me", no "book a call", no "GreenM solves this". This is Alexey's voice, not a sales channel.
5. **Language variant consistency.** Confirm one variant throughout (British or International English, see Register section for decision rule). No mixing within a single piece. If British, consistent use of organisation, behaviour, recognise, programme, centre, licence (noun), labour. If International, avoid strong UK markers (whilst, amongst, programme as "schedule", lift, queue).
6. **Verifiable claims.** Any number, study, or factual claim must be either verifiable from source material, framed as personal observation ("in my experience", "from what I've seen"), or removed.

### Tier 2 — Fix If Time Permits

These are quality checks. The content can publish without them, but it will be weaker.

7. Does it read as written by someone who has been inside this — or by someone who has read about it?
8. Is there at least one specific, concrete operational detail that grounds the observation?
9. Does it end with genuine uncertainty or an open question — or does it rush to a resolution?
10. Are Tier 2 high-risk words (robust, navigate, foster, landscape, etc.) each grounded in a concrete operational detail in the same or following sentence?

### Tier 3 — Calibration Across Multiple Pieces

These are pattern-level checks. Run them periodically (weekly or after every three pieces), not on every individual draft.

11. Do recent pieces share an opener pattern? If yes, the next piece must break it.
12. Do recent pieces share a closer type (declarative inversion, open question, unresolved tension)? If yes, vary.
13. Does the emotional register match the weight of the subject — or has every piece been written at the same tempo regardless of topic?

## Voice Maintenance — Keeping This Guide Useful Over Time

A voice guide that never updates becomes a constraint instead of a tool. Markets shift, audiences change, what worked in 2025 stops working in 2027. But a voice guide that updates too eagerly loses its anchor — every weak draft can rationalise a rule change. The process below sets a deliberate cadence for review and a small set of triggers that justify pulling rules out of cycle.

*This is a generic template. Adapt the roles, cadences, and inputs to match your actual working reality.*

### Three Loops, Three Cadences

The guide is maintained through three loops with different speeds and different triggers.

**Loop 1 — Per-edit reactive (always-on).**
When a specific rule blocks a piece of content that should clearly work, flag the case immediately. Do not silently override the rule in the draft, and do not silently follow it at the cost of the piece. Both options corrupt the guide — the first by treating it as advisory, the second by treating it as inflexible. Instead: note the rule, the case where it failed, and the suggested fix in a running log (a single document, a Slack channel, a Linear issue queue — whatever holds friction lowest). The log is reviewed at Loop 2 cadence. This loop has no decision rights — it captures cases, it does not change rules.

**Loop 2 — Pattern review (monthly).**
Once a month, the person responsible for this guide reviews:
- The reactive log from Loop 1 (all flagged rule-failures from the past month)
- The Tier 3 calibration checks from the Self-Check across all content produced (opener patterns, closer patterns, register-subject match)
- The Marginal Calibration Examples section against actual drafts edited — has the team caught any new failure modes that aren't yet documented?

For each item, decide one of three outcomes:
1. *Rule stands, edit the draft.* The rule is correct; the draft was wrong; no guide change.
2. *Rule needs clarification or scope refinement.* Add an exception, narrow the scope, or note a context where the rule doesn't apply.
3. *Rule needs to change or be removed.* Rare. Requires evidence from multiple cases, not a single edit.

Changes from Loop 2 are made directly in the guide with a brief commit message or change note describing what changed and why.

**Loop 3 — Performance review (quarterly).**
Once a quarter, review the guide against external signals — not edits, but outcomes.

Inputs include any of the following that are available:
- Engagement metrics on published content (impressions, comments, comment quality)
- Feedback from readers, prospects, partners on specific pieces
- Anything the team has observed about what is working and what is being ignored
- Win/loss data for commercial content where attribution is plausible

The question at Loop 3 is different: not "did this draft pass the rules", but "are these rules still pointing at what we want the voice to do?" If the diagnostic stance reliably gets more engagement than alternative framings, the rule is doing its job. If posts that bend a rule consistently outperform posts that follow it, the rule needs scrutiny.

Loop 3 can result in larger structural changes — adding a new section, deprecating one, reworking the relationship between voices. Changes from Loop 3 are made with a longer note in the guide explaining the data behind the decision, so the rationale stays visible.

### Decision Rights

For each loop, name:
- Who can **flag** an item (Loops 1, 2): anyone producing content
- Who can **review** and decide (Loops 2, 3): the named guide owner, typically the person closest to both the writing and the strategy
- Who must **approve** changes that affect voice character (not style mechanics): the principal whose voice this is — for this guide, Alexey personally signs off on changes to diagnostic stance, authority sources, or emotional register principles. Style-mechanical changes (adding to forbidden lists, updating thresholds, clarifying scope) do not require this approval.

The split matters because it prevents the guide from drifting on small accumulations of decisions made by people who weren't part of defining the voice in the first place. Voice character is the principal's responsibility; the guide is just the place it gets written down.

### What to Avoid

The most common failure modes in voice-guide maintenance:

- **Loop 1 becoming silent.** People stop logging the friction because logging feels bureaucratic. The guide stops getting accurate inputs. Counter: keep the log radically lightweight. One sentence per case. No template required.
- **Loop 2 becoming defensive.** Reviews where every flagged case results in "rule stands, edit the draft" — even when the same case appears repeatedly. This is the guide hardening against legitimate signal. Counter: any case that has appeared three times in the log must result in option 2 or 3, not option 1.
- **Loop 3 over-reacting to single metrics.** One post outperforms by 3x, the team rewrites a rule. The variance was probably noise. Counter: structural changes require evidence from at least three independent cases, not one outlier.
- **Treating the guide as final.** Voice guides earn their authority through use, not through completeness. A guide that is missing sections (format coverage, calibration examples, feedback loops) but is honest about what it covers is more useful than a guide that pretends to cover everything.

The goal is not a perfect document. The goal is a document that stays useful as the writing gets done.
