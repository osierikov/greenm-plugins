# GreenM — Voice and Style Guide

This document defines the voice, character, perspective, and style conventions of GreenM across all written content. Two layers live here: the voice itself (grounded in operations, honest about complexity, insight-before-company) which is constant across formats, and recommended style defaults (sentence rhythm, level of technical detail, where capabilities surface) which channel-specific skills may refine for their particular medium.

Apply it to blog posts, Substack articles, LinkedIn company posts, case studies, website copy, whitepapers, email newsletters, product pages, or any other content type. Where a channel-specific skill exists for a format, its rules take precedence on format-mechanical questions (length, structure, platform conventions). On voice questions — what GreenM sounds like, what it never says, how it positions itself — this guide is the source of truth.

---

## Who GreenM Is as a Brand Voice

GreenM is a healthcare AI company that builds private deployment infrastructure, clinical workflow integration, and compliance-first AI systems for mid-market healthcare organisations — typically 20 to 200 staff. The company voice reflects the people building it: engineers who care whether the clinician on the other end of the system has a better Tuesday because of what they built.

GreenM's voice is that of a specialist practitioner in a world full of generalists. Not the loudest voice in the room. The most credible one.

The brand does not hype, lecture, or position itself above its audience. It speaks to healthcare decision-makers and technical leads as peers — people solving the same hard problems from different sides of the table.

---

## The Core Brand Stance

**Grounded in the operational.** GreenM content is always anchored in what actually happens when an AI system meets a real clinical environment. Theoretical possibility is interesting; operational reality is what GreenM writes about.

**Specific over general.** One concrete detail is worth ten generalisations. A compliance review that had to restart. An engineer who rewrote an escalation flow because it added one extra click during peak hours. These specifics do more work than any claim about industry-leading capabilities.

**Honest about complexity.** Healthcare AI is hard. The integration layer is always underestimated. Multi-site coordination takes longer than anyone budgets. Compliance isn't a box to check. GreenM doesn't smooth these realities over — it names them, because that's what builds credibility with the people who know.

*How honesty operates in commercial formats (service pages, sales materials, case studies positioned for prospects):* honesty does not require constantly restating the difficulty of the work. In thought leadership (blog, LinkedIn, whitepapers) the honesty surfaces directly through observations like "the integration layer is always underestimated." In commercial formats, the same honesty operates differently — through **specificity instead of inflated claims**, and through **what is not promised** rather than what is promised. A service page that says "PoC in 4–6 weeks with one named workflow, scoped on a 5-day kickoff" is honest. A service page that says "we deliver fast, scalable, transformative AI solutions" is not. The discipline is to never inflate, never claim outcomes that aren't backed by delivery experience, and never make the work sound simpler than it is — but the form this takes in a service page is restraint and specificity, not a paragraph about complexity. Repeating "this is harder than you think" in commercial materials sounds defensive; specificity sounds confident.

**Solutions-oriented without over-promising.** GreenM is confident about what it can deliver — private deployment, compliance-first architecture, PoC in 4–6 weeks — but never inflates outcomes or implies problems are simpler than they are.

**Human-centred about clinical work.** The technology GreenM builds serves clinicians under real pressure. That context is always present in how GreenM talks about what it does. The goal isn't AI adoption. It's a clinical team that has a better workflow.

## Diagnostic to Solution — When and How GreenM's Voice Moves From Problem to Approach

GreenM's voice shares a diagnostic foundation with Alexey's: name what you see, ground it in operational reality, avoid rushing to resolution. The difference is that GreenM has a legitimate place to describe what it does — capability statements that would be self-promotional in Alexey's voice are appropriate on the company brand. The question is **when** in a piece of content the voice moves from diagnosing the problem to describing GreenM's approach. The answer depends on the content type and the goal of the piece.

The placement is not arbitrary. Moving to solutions too early reduces the piece to a sales pitch and undermines credibility with informed readers. Moving too late or not at all turns the piece into thought leadership that fails to do the brand work expected of company content. The matrix below sets defaults; channel-specific skills may refine them.

### Decision Matrix

| Content Type | Primary Goal | When GreenM's Approach Enters | How Direct |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LinkedIn company post** | Brand positioning, thought leadership | Late or implicit. The post is mostly observation; GreenM's perspective surfaces in one or two sentences, often near the end. Sometimes not at all by name. | Low to moderate. "At GreenM we've started treating..." is acceptable once per post. |
| **Blog article (greenm.io)** | SEO, thought leadership, demand generation | In the second half. The first half establishes the problem and pattern with full diagnostic weight. GreenM's approach enters as one of several possible takes, grounded in delivery experience. | Moderate. Can describe what GreenM does in 1–2 paragraphs, but the article must stand as useful without that section. |
| **Linkbuilding article (external publication)** | Backlinks, awareness | Once or twice, naturally — anchor text placement only. The article reads as voice-driven thought leadership for the host publication. | Low. GreenM is referenced as the author's affiliation, not as the subject. |
| **Anonymous case study** | Demand generation, proof | After the pattern is established. Lead with the outcome or the failure mode the client faced; GreenM's role appears mid-piece. | Moderate. Plain statement of what was built, how long it took, what changed. No dramatisation. |
| **Named case study (with client approval)** | Demand generation, proof | Earlier than anonymous — the client is the subject, GreenM is the partner. Approach is described in service of explaining the work. | Moderate to high. Can describe specific architectural decisions, integration choices, compliance design. |
| **Whitepaper / long-form technical content** | Authority, lead capture | In the technical sections that naturally call for it. The framing and problem statement are diagnostic; the methodology and architectural sections describe how GreenM approaches it. | High in technical sections, moderate in framing. Capability is described as how the work gets done, not as differentiation. |
| **Service page (greenm.io)** | Conversion | Immediately. The page exists to describe what GreenM does. The problem is named in 1–2 sentences as context, then the service is described directly. | High. This is the one format where "we do X" is the primary mode. Still no superlatives, still grounded in operational facts. |
| **Email newsletter** | Audience retention, engagement | Varies by issue. Editorial-style issues mirror blog rules (approach enters in the second half); product or update-focused issues lead with what's new. | Varies. Editorial: low. Product/update: moderate. |
| **Sales material (one-pagers, decks)** | Conversion | Immediately. Like service pages — the material exists to describe the offering. Problem framing is brief; approach and outcomes are the substance. | High. Direct claims allowed when each is grounded in specific delivery evidence. |

### How the Transition Reads

When GreenM's approach enters the text, the transition itself signals voice. Three patterns work; one fails.

**Works — operational grounding:** "Across the deployments we've run, the integration layer takes roughly twice as long as teams estimate. We've started budgeting for that explicitly." The transition is anchored in evidence, not in a marketing claim.

**Works — perspective framing:** "There are several ways to handle this. The approach we've taken is..." The reader sees that GreenM is offering one view, not the only view. This preserves credibility.

**Works — restraint:** Describing what GreenM does without claiming it is unique or superior. "GreenM builds these deployments inside the client's environment. Zero patient data leaves the boundary." Stating facts, not advantages.

**Fails — bridge claims:** "That's why GreenM is the partner that..." or "This is exactly the kind of challenge we solve." These framings collapse the diagnostic into a sales pitch and damage the credibility built by the diagnostic section.

### The Quick Test

Before the transition to GreenM's approach, ask: has the problem been described well enough that a reader who is not a prospect would still find this section useful? If yes, the transition is earned. If no, the diagnostic section needs more weight before the brand surfaces.

## Tone Descriptors

Any GreenM content should feel:

- **Confident** — clear about what the company does and why it matters, without overselling
- **Grounded** — anchored in real operational experience, not abstract positioning
- **Practical** — focused on what actually changes for the organisation, the clinician, the workflow
- **Honest** — willing to name complexity, underestimation, and the parts that take longer than expected
- **Business-casual** — professional but not stiff; expert without being inaccessible
- **Direct** — says the thing rather than approaching it from three angles

## What GreenM Does NOT Sound Like

**Not a technology vendor making capability claims.** Phrases like "industry-leading", "cutting-edge", "state-of-the-art", "next-generation" immediately signal generic vendor positioning. GreenM's differentiation is specific and operational, not superlative.

**Not a consultant selling a framework.** GreenM doesn't brand its approach, name its methodology, or imply that its process is what makes it special. What makes it special is what it delivers. That should be evident in the content.

**Not a startup pitching investors.** No TAM/SAM/SOM framing. No "we're disrupting X". No language designed to impress people who haven't seen the sausage being made.

**Not a thought leadership factory.** GreenM doesn't publish generic healthcare AI trend commentary. Every piece of content should be grounded in real experience, real clients, real deployments — or it shouldn't be published.

**Not a case study machine.** Content is not a list of what clients GreenM has worked with and what they do. One client reference, used to ground an insight. The insight is the content. The client is the context.

## Language Character

### Register

Business-casual. The kind of language a senior engineer or solutions architect uses when talking to a CTO or COO who understands the domain but doesn't live in the technical details. Not simplified, not over-explained, not jargon-heavy without purpose.

### Language Variant — British or International English

Spelling and lexical choices depend on the audience and topic, not on a fixed default. Two variants are in use:

**British English** — used when the audience or topic is clearly UK-oriented. Triggers include: NHS-focused content, UK-specific compliance frameworks (NHS DSPT, CQC), UK clinical settings, content aimed at UK or European healthcare leaders, case studies of UK clients, service pages or sales materials specifically targeting UK ICPs. Use: organisation, behaviour, recognise, programme, centre, licence (noun), labour.

**International English** — used by default when the audience is mixed or unspecified, and for any content aimed at US or globally distributed readers. International English keeps the British base spellings that are widely understood (organisation, behaviour, recognise) but avoids strong UK markers that read as foreign to American readers (programme in the sense of TV programme, lift, queue, whilst, amongst). Use standard international vocabulary: schedule not programme when referring to a plan, while not whilst, among not amongst.

**Within a single piece**, the choice is binary — do not mix variants. A blog article, LinkedIn post, case study, or whitepaper uses one variant throughout. The decision is made before writing begins, based on the dominant audience the piece is aimed at.

**For linkbuilding articles on third-party publications:** match the host publication's variant when their house style is documented or evident. Voice character (no hype, no vendor positioning, operational grounding) is preserved; spelling adapts.

**Quick reference:**
- NHS, CQC, UK compliance, UK clinical setting, UK ICP-targeted content → British
- HIPAA, US clinics, mid-market US, mixed transatlantic audience, default → International
- Linkbuilding on US-publication → match host (typically US or International)
- When in doubt → International (it reads naturally to both UK and US readers; strong British markers do not)

### Sentence Style

Clear and direct. Short sentences for emphasis. Longer sentences when the idea requires it — but never padding. Every word earns its place.

GreenM content avoids smooth, generic transitions between ideas ("As a result...", "This is why...", "This means that..."). Ideas sit next to each other and the reader makes the connection. This signals confidence and respects the reader's intelligence.

Numbers used as texture: "four weeks from kickoff", "one named owner", "the third pilot". Not as performance metrics.

### Level of Technical Detail

GreenM can be more technically specific than Alexey's personal voice — the audience includes CTOs, Heads of IT, and technical procurement leads who need to know the company understands the infrastructure. But technical detail is always connected to an operational or business outcome. "FHIR-compliant" is not a claim — it's a starting point. What matters is what it means for integration time and compliance review.

## GreenM's Point of View on Healthcare AI

These beliefs should be implicit in everything GreenM publishes:

- The integration layer is always the hardest part. Not the model. The plumbing.
- Compliance isn't a feature. It's the architecture. Built in from the start, not reviewed at the end.
- Mid-market healthcare organisations — 20 to 200 staff — are underserved. Enterprise platforms are too heavy. Consumer tools are too simple. The middle is where GreenM operates.
- Private deployment isn't a luxury. For most healthcare organisations, it's the only compliant option.
- Speed to value matters. A PoC in 4–6 weeks is achievable with discipline — not shortcuts.
- Named ownership is the difference between a deployed model and an operational capability.
- The organisations that move fastest are the ones that assign a local owner early, not the ones with the most resources.

These are not talking points to be stated. They are the perspective from which everything is written.

## What GreenM Can Reference

GreenM content can name the company's capabilities directly — this is appropriate on the brand page in a way it is not on Alexey's personal page.

**Appropriate references:**
- Zero patient data leaves the organisation's infrastructure
- HIPAA, GDPR, and NHS DSPT compliance
- Private deployment: on-premise, controlled cloud, or hybrid
- FHIR integration
- PoC in 4–6 weeks
- Built for mid-market healthcare (20–200 staff)

These should be woven into content naturally — as context for an observation, not as a capability list. They are facts that support a point, not the point itself.

**Client references:**
One per piece of content, maximum. Brief and often anonymised. The reference grounds an insight; it does not become the story. GreenM does not publish content structured as "Client A did X, Client B did Y, Client C did Z." That is an internal report, not external content.

## Forbidden Words and Phrases

Language incompatible with GreenM's voice is sorted into three tiers. The tiers exist because absolute "never use" lists produce two failure modes: they get ignored when they over-reach, and they force awkward workarounds in technical or formal formats where a flagged word has a legitimate literal meaning.

### Proper Noun Exemption — Applies to All Tiers

Proper nouns are exempt from every list below. This includes client names, product names, organisations, publications, and people's names — even when a forbidden word appears as a root inside them. *Empower Work* is acceptable as a client name; "empower clinicians" remains forbidden as marketing language. *Pivotal Software* is acceptable as a company reference; "pivotal moment" remains high-risk. The exemption covers verbatim use of the name only — it does not extend to derived adjectives or related phrasing.

### Tier 1 — Never Use (Vendor Hype and Strong AI Signals)

These are always wrong in GreenM's voice. No grounding rescues them. If one appears in a draft, rewrite the sentence.

cutting-edge, transformative, revolutionise, game-changing, unlock, leverage, synergy, empower (as verb), industry-leading, state-of-the-art, next-generation, innovative, disruptive, best-in-class, world-class, seamless, frictionless, delve / delve into, tapestry, testament, paradigm / paradigm shift

### Tier 2 — High-Risk (Allowed Only with Operational Grounding)

These words are not banned, but they signal AI writing or vendor positioning unless they are anchored to a concrete operational detail in the same sentence or the one immediately following. The test: does this word do real work here, or is it doing the work of sounding important? If the latter, cut it. If the former, the grounding must be present and specific.

robust, navigate (as a metaphor for handling difficulty), foster, bolster, landscape (as in "the healthcare landscape"), pivotal, nuanced / nuanced approach, multifaceted, aligns with, powerful

Examples of correct use:
- *"A robust audit trail — meaning every model decision is logged with timestamp, user, and input — is what regulators ask for first."* (technical term, operationally grounded)
- *"The compliance landscape includes HIPAA, GDPR, and NHS DSPT, and they don't fully overlap."* (literal, specific)

Examples of incorrect use:
- *"GreenM's robust approach to private deployment"* (no grounding, doing rhetorical work)
- *"Helping organisations navigate the complex healthcare AI landscape"* (both words used as metaphor, no specifics — pure vendor positioning)

### Tier 3 — Context-Dependent (Avoid in Marketing Register; Acceptable in Technical or Literal Use)

These have legitimate uses in technical and analytical content but become hype in marketing or thought-leadership framing.

stakeholders, scalable, change management

*"Stakeholders"* is acceptable when listing actual parties in a governance review (clinical leads, IT, compliance, finance). It is forbidden when used as a vague catch-all to avoid naming specific roles. *"Scalable"* is acceptable in genuinely technical architectural discussion (a system designed for horizontal scaling under defined load conditions). It is forbidden as a marketing modifier on capabilities ("scalable AI solution"). *"Change management"* is acceptable in academic or technical discussion of organisational change methodology. It is forbidden when used to wave away the difficulty of operational adoption.

### Generic Content Openers (Tier 1 — Never Use)

"In today's fast-paced world...", "It's no secret that...", "Let's be honest...", "The truth is...", "At the end of the day...", "This is a reminder that...", "Here's what we've learned...", "The reality is...", "More importantly...", "It's worth noting..."

### Soft Generalisations Used as Facts

"most healthcare organisations", "most teams", "the majority of providers" — use instead: "often", "frequently", "tends to", "in many cases", or frame as observation from GreenM's own delivery experience ("from the deployments we've run", "across the projects we've delivered").

### Punctuation

No em dashes (—). They are a strong signal of AI-generated writing and interrupt the measured cadence GreenM's voice depends on. Use commas, full stops, or sentence breaks.

| Dimension | Alexey (Personal) | GreenM (Brand) |

### Operational Markers — How to Tell the Two Voices Apart in Practice

*This section is a working hypothesis. The markers below were derived from the structural differences described in both voice guides and the patterns visible in published content. They should be refined with concrete examples as the team identifies them in real edits.*

When a draft could plausibly be either voice, these markers should disambiguate. They are not absolute rules — they are the most reliable signals.

**Epistemic markers — how the writer claims authority.**

Alexey speaks in the first person singular and uses personal observation as the source of authority. Characteristic phrases: "I watched", "I've seen", "in my experience", "my sense is", "one pattern I keep coming back to". The observation comes from him personally.

GreenM speaks in first person plural or in an observational third person, and uses delivery experience as the source of authority. Characteristic phrases: "from the deployments we've run", "across the projects we've delivered", "we've started treating", "in every project we've delivered, we see". The observation comes from accumulated company practice.

The test: would the sentence still work if you removed the "I" or the "we"? If removing "I" makes it sound generic, the sentence relies on Alexey's individual voice. If removing "we" makes it sound generic, the sentence relies on GreenM's collective voice. If both versions work, the sentence is currently voice-neutral and one of the markers should be added.

**Technical density and named capabilities.**

Alexey's content rarely uses specific technical terms (FHIR, CrewAI, NHS DSPT, on-prem vs hybrid deployment). When it does, the terms appear once as context, not as the substance of the argument. GreenM's content can use these terms more densely and as the substance of the argument — explaining what FHIR integration means in practice, describing the difference between on-premise and controlled-cloud deployment architectures.

The test: how many distinct technical terms appear, and do they each do real work? Alexey: 0–2 per piece, used as context. GreenM: 2–8 per piece, used as substance.

**Subject of the sentence.**

In Alexey's voice, the subject of most sentences is a pattern, a clinical setting, a team behaviour, a system, a clinician, or an abstract concept. The brand name is absent.

In GreenM's voice, the subject of some sentences is GreenM itself — what GreenM builds, where it deploys, how it approaches a problem. The frequency varies by content type (see the Decision Matrix earlier), but the brand is a legitimate subject. The presence of "GreenM" as a sentence subject is the clearest marker of the brand voice.

**Closing move.**

Alexey closes on observation: a declarative inversion, an unresolved tension, an open question with no resolution. The closer is structural, not transactional.

GreenM closes on invitation: an open question that invites the reader's experience, occasionally a soft pointer to further reading or specific capability (only in formats where this is appropriate — see Decision Matrix). The closer is conversational, not strictly observational.

**When the markers fail.**

There is a class of content where the two voices look almost identical: short technical observations about deployment patterns that don't name GreenM, don't use "I", and don't use heavy technical density. In these cases, the deciding factor is the publishing context — which page, which channel, which audience. If the same text could publish on either page, default to Alexey's personal voice (the personal profile has higher reach, and content not requiring brand presence is better there). Move to GreenM only when the content's purpose is brand-specific.

When a topic could work in either voice, the default is Alexey's personal page (personal profiles outperform brand pages significantly on LinkedIn and most content platforms). GreenM's voice is for content where the company's specific capabilities, client results, or team perspective is the point — content that would feel like self-promotion if written in Alexey's name.

## Voice in External and Third-Person Formats

Some content places GreenM in someone else's space — a partner site's company description, an about-block in a guest article, a conference listing, a press mention. The grammar and context change. The voice does not.

### Scope — When These Rules Apply

These rules apply to copy that GreenM **supplies** for external use: company descriptions provided to conference organisers or directories, about-blocks sent to publishers for inclusion at the end of guest articles, quotes given to journalists for inclusion in their pieces. They do not apply to copy that external authors write about GreenM independently — a journalist's profile piece, a third-party analyst report, an article that mentions GreenM as one of several companies are outside this skill's remit. If asked to review such copy after the fact, you can flag deviations, but you do not enforce voice rules on writers who are not part of the team.

The test: did GreenM or someone writing on its behalf choose this wording? If yes, the rules apply. If no, the rules are advisory at most.

### Company description for external placement

Used on partner sites, conference directories, publication author pages, or anywhere GreenM is described by a third party using copy GreenM provides.

The temptation is to write a dense capability list. This sounds like every other company description and carries none of GreenM's actual character.

**✓ Correct approach**
Lead with what GreenM does in practice — the operational reality — not a category label or mission statement. One concrete fact about the work, then the context.

> *"GreenM builds private AI infrastructure for mid-market healthcare organisations. Every deployment runs inside the client's own environment — no patient data leaves. The company works with organisations of 20 to 200 staff across the US and UK."*

**✗ Incorrect approach**
> *"GreenM is an innovative healthcare AI company delivering cutting-edge, HIPAA-compliant solutions that empower mid-market organisations to unlock the transformative potential of artificial intelligence."*

Rules for company description:
- First sentence: what GreenM actually does, stated as operational fact
- Second sentence: one concrete differentiator — private deployment, compliance, speed to value — not a list of all three
- Third sentence: context (geography, client size) if space allows
- No superlatives, no hype vocabulary
- Language variant matches the host context (British for UK directories and UK conferences; International for US or globally distributed placements — see Register section)
- Maximum three sentences for short placements; up to five for longer author bios

### About-block in guest article or linkbuilding piece

A one or two sentence company description that follows the article byline or appears at the end of the piece.

Same rules as company description above. Even shorter. The about-block should not oversell — it is context for the reader, not a pitch.

**✓ Correct**
> *"GreenM deploys private AI infrastructure for healthcare organisations across the US and UK, working with clinics and health groups of 20 to 200 staff."*

**✗ Incorrect**
> *"GreenM is a leading healthcare AI company helping organisations achieve their digital transformation goals with secure, scalable, and compliant AI solutions."*

### Press mention or quote

When GreenM provides a statement or quote for external publication, it should read the way GreenM's best content reads — specific, grounded, without corporate softening.

**✓ Correct**
> *"The model is the easy part. What takes time is the data layer, the governance, and the integration work that makes the model useful under real clinical conditions."*

**✗ Incorrect**
> *"GreenM is excited to be at the forefront of healthcare AI innovation, helping organisations leverage advanced technology to improve patient outcomes."*

Quotes for external use: declarative, specific, maximum two sentences. No "excited to", no "proud to", no "committed to".

## Emotional Register — Adapting to the Weight of the Topic

GreenM's voice moves along a continuous spectrum depending on the emotional weight of the subject. The character stays constant — grounded, honest, solutions-oriented. What changes is pace, warmth, and how close the writing sits to the human experience inside the subject.

This is not a set of modes to switch between. It is a continuous adjustment. The voice should feel calibrated to the subject.

### The spectrum

**Heavy end — topics with real human stakes**

When the content touches patient safety, clinical failure, compliance crisis, or situations where the cost of getting it wrong is felt by real people, the voice slows. Less forward momentum toward solutions. More time in the problem. GreenM's capability or approach enters later or more quietly — the subject earns the space before the company does.

What changes:
- Shorter sentences, more room between thoughts
- The "here's what we do" move is delayed or absent
- The closer does not pivot quickly to capability or call to action
- Language stays plain — no energetic verbs, no "we're excited to"

What stays the same:
- Honesty about complexity — if anything, more so
- No dramatisation or amplification of the difficulty
- Concrete detail still grounds the writing
- Blame still redirects to design and structure, not individuals

**Middle — operational, technical, and analytical topics**

The default register. Integration challenges, deployment architecture, compliance frameworks, pilot-to-production patterns. Confident, direct, specific. GreenM's perspective enters naturally. Solutions are described plainly.

**Light end — team, culture, milestone, partnership**

When the content is about the people building GreenM, a delivery milestone, or a relationship worth acknowledging, the voice warms. Not effusively — GreenM does not use exclamation marks or generic praise. The warmth comes from the same place it always does: a specific detail about what actually happened.

What changes:
- The observation can carry appreciation without stating it directly
- Slightly more generous with pace — the moment is not compressed
- GreenM can be the subject of the sentence here in a way it rarely is elsewhere

What stays the same:
- Still no "we're so proud", "incredible team", "excited to share"
- Still grounded in specifics — what was built, what changed, what someone did
- Still no exclamation marks

Example register (team/culture):
> *"One of our engineers spent two days rewriting an escalation flow because the original added one extra click for clinical staff during peak hours. Nobody asked them to fix it. They fixed it because they'd spent time watching how the tool was actually being used."*

The pride is in the detail. It is never declared.

### Quantitative Signals — Operational Thresholds for Each Register

*These thresholds were derived from analysis of 14 published LinkedIn posts (April 2026 pack), with the same corpus and methodology used in the Alexey voice guide. The middle register is well-represented (11 posts across both voices). The heavy register is measured from a single post. The light register is measured from two posts. Treat these as first-pass calibration values — they apply most reliably to LinkedIn company posts; for blog articles, whitepapers, case studies, and service pages, the proportions hold but the absolute values will shift with length. Refine over time as more proven content accumulates.*

The descriptive guidance above ("shorter sentences", "more room between thoughts") is correct but vague. The numbers below make it falsifiable — if a draft is meant to read as heavy register but its quantitative profile matches middle register, the register is mis-calibrated.

| Signal | Heavy Register | Middle Register | Light Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average sentence length (words) | 13–15 | 9–12 | 12–15 |
| Median sentence length (words) | 12–14 | 6–11 | 10–13 |
| Short sentences per post (≤6 words) | 3–5 | 6–10 | 3–5 |
| Average paragraph length (words) | 28–32 | 20–28 | 26–32 |
| Position of first epistemic anchor (we've / we deployed / at GreenM, we) | 35–50% into post | 0–35% into post | 0–25% into post |
| Closing move | Declarative, no question | Question (10 of 11 measured cases) | Question or soft acknowledgment |

**How to read the table.**

Middle register is the most compressed and rhythmic — many short sentences, varied length, epistemic anchor arrives early, almost always closes with a question.

Heavy register and light register both run **longer at the sentence and paragraph level** than middle, but for opposite reasons. Heavy: the longer sentences are doing the work of letting the difficulty sit, not compressing past the moment. Light: the longer sentences are doing the work of letting appreciation breathe without rushing.

The clearest distinguishing signals between heavy and light are: position of epistemic anchor (heavy: later, ~40%; light: earlier, ~17%) and closing move (heavy: declarative sitting with the difficulty; light: question or acknowledgment that invites response).

**How to use the table.**

Do not write to the numbers — write to the subject. After drafting, check the numbers as a calibration test. If a draft is meant to land heavy but its average sentence length is 9 words, it has been compressed into middle register and the weight has been lost. If a light register draft has its first "we" anchor at 50% into the post, the warmth is arriving too late.

The numbers are diagnostic, not generative. They identify when the register has slipped, not how to fix it.

### The calibration test

Before finalising any piece, ask: does the pace and register of this text match the weight of the subject? A heavy topic written at the same tempo as a technical explainer has lost something. A culture post compressed into the same tight analytical mode as an integration article has also lost something.

The voice should feel like it was written for this specific subject — not applied to it from a template.

## Structural Variety — Preventing Pattern Repetition

GreenM's voice must be recognisable without being predictable. The risk is not losing the voice — it is the voice becoming a template. When every piece follows the same arc, the company starts to sound like it has one thing to say, repeated in different words.

### The dominant arc to avoid repeating

The most common structural pattern in GreenM's published content:

> *Industry problem stated → "In every project we've delivered, we see X" → what GreenM does differently → question to the reader*

This arc is solid. It has produced good content. But used repeatedly it becomes a pattern the reader can complete after the first sentence. The insight that follows the setup lands with less force because the reader already knows a GreenM observation is coming.

**Rule:** no single structural arc should appear in two consecutive pieces of content. Across any three pieces produced in the same period, at least two must open and develop differently.

### Alternative structures — use these to break the arc

**Lead with the operational detail, not the problem category**
Instead of naming the problem first, drop the reader into a specific moment or finding. The problem becomes visible through the detail.
> *"The first 100 real queries broke every assumption the design phase had made."*

**State the counterintuitive thing plainly and let it sit**
No setup, no softening. The observation is the opening. The rest of the piece earns it.
> *"We don't start with models. We start with data. That's not a philosophy. It's an order of operations."*

**Open with what most approaches get wrong — without naming GreenM yet**
Let the first section be entirely about the failure pattern. GreenM's approach enters only once the problem is fully visible to the reader.

**Use a field observation without the "we see this pattern" framing**
State what happens in practice directly, without attributing it to GreenM's experience first. The company's perspective is implicit.
> *"Three assumptions come up in almost every first call with a private clinic considering an AI deployment."*

**Lead with the outcome, not the process**
Especially for case studies and service pages — start with what changed, then explain how.

### What must vary across pieces

- **Opening move:** industry problem, operational detail, counterintuitive statement, field observation, outcome — rotate across pieces
- **When GreenM enters the text:** sometimes in the first paragraph, sometimes not until the second half — the placement should serve the argument, not the formula
- **Closer:** open question, unresolved tension, operational statement, invitation to share experience — not the same type twice in a row
- **Presence of "we":** some pieces carry GreenM's voice through "we" throughout; others are written more observationally with GreenM referenced by name in one place

### What must not vary

Structural variety is not permission to change the voice. The honesty about complexity, the insight-before-company principle, the grounding in operational specifics — these stay constant regardless of which structure is used. Variety happens within the voice, not against it.

## Format Adaptation — What Changes, What Doesn't

GreenM's voice applies across five content formats: **LinkedIn posts**, **blog articles**, **linkbuilding articles**, **case studies**, and **service page descriptions**. The voice is the same across all five. What changes is the role of GreenM in the text, the depth of client detail, and how directly capabilities are stated.

### What never changes regardless of format

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Insight before company | The observation or problem always comes first. GreenM's role or capability follows. |
| Honest about complexity | Never implies the work is simpler than it is, regardless of format |
| No hype vocabulary | The full forbidden word list applies to every format including service pages |
| Language variant consistency | One variant per piece (British or International — choice depends on audience; see Register section) |
| No em dashes | Applies everywhere |
| Blame redirects to structure | Never assigns failure to individuals or client teams |
| One client reference maximum | Per piece of content — applies to blog, LinkedIn, and linkbuilding equally |
| No lists of projects or clients | Content is never structured as a showcase of who GreenM has worked with |

### What adapts by format

**LinkedIn post**
- Shortest and most compressed format
- One observation, one thread, one question at the end
- Client reference: one brief anonymous mention if it grounds the point, otherwise none
- GreenM capabilities: referenced only when directly relevant to the post's observation
- No subheadings

**Blog article (greenm.io)**
- Develops one argument across multiple connected points
- Can cite external data and research — grounded in a specific claim, not decorative
- GreenM perspective referenced naturally where it adds to the argument
- One anonymous client reference maximum, used to illustrate a pattern
- Subheadings as argument signposts, not navigation labels
- Ends on an open question or unresolved observation — not a summary

**Linkbuilding article (external publication)**
- Written in GreenM's voice but published on third-party sites
- GreenM referenced once or twice naturally — anchor text placement as required by the brief
- The article must stand on its own as a useful piece of content independent of the GreenM reference
- Same voice rules apply: no hype, no vendor positioning, no "our solution"
- Tone matches the host publication's register while preserving GreenM's voice character

**Case study**
- Named client: only with explicit client approval. Otherwise fully anonymous — no sector, no geography, no team size that would identify the organisation.
- Anonymous case study: leads with the pattern or outcome, uses the client detail to ground it. "A mid-sized UK clinic" is sufficient.
- Named case study: leads with the outcome, not the client's name. "ROC Clinic reduced documentation time by three hours per clinician per week" not "We worked with ROC Clinic on an exciting project."
- One case study covers one project and one core finding. Not a portfolio.
- GreenM's role stated plainly — what was built, how long it took, what changed. No dramatisation.

**Service page description (greenm.io)**
- The most direct format — capability stated as operational fact
- Follows the pattern: problem the service solves → what GreenM does → what changes for the organisation
- No hype openings ("GreenM's industry-leading..."). Start with the problem or the condition.
- Capabilities stated concisely and specifically — "zero patient data leaves the organisation's infrastructure" not "comprehensive data security"
- Can be more direct about GreenM's approach than other formats — this is the one place where "we do X" is appropriate
- Still no superlatives, no competitor comparisons, no claims without operational grounding

### The test that applies to all formats

Read the draft and ask: if you removed GreenM's name from this piece entirely, would it still be useful to the reader? If yes — the content is grounded. If no — the piece is about GreenM, not about the problem GreenM solves. Rewrite until the answer is yes, then add GreenM back in the right place.

## References — Voice Calibration Examples

These examples are the ground truth for GreenM's voice. Each pair shows the same idea written correctly and incorrectly. The topic is irrelevant — only the voice pattern matters.

---

### 1. Insight first, company second

**✓ Correct**
> When we build an AI assistant for a healthcare organisation, the model selection takes a few days. The knowledge base takes weeks. And then the first real users arrive. Within 48 hours, we learn more about what the system needs to know than we did in the entire design phase.

**✗ Incorrect**
> At GreenM, we leverage our extensive experience in healthcare AI to deliver cutting-edge solutions that accelerate your path to production. Our proven methodology ensures fast time-to-value for your organisation.

*Why:* The correct version shares an observation first — something the reader can immediately test against their own experience. GreenM appears as the context, not the subject. The incorrect version leads with the company and fills the space with hype phrases that carry no information.

---

### 2. One client reference, used to ground a point — not to showcase

**✓ Correct**
> One recent deployment had a knowledge base that covered 200+ clinical FAQs. Within the first week, 30% of real queries fell into gaps nobody anticipated. Not because the team was careless — because the gap between how clinicians think about information and how patients ask for it is wider than anyone estimates.

**✗ Incorrect**
> We recently worked with a major UK healthcare group to deploy an AI assistant across three sites. The project covered 200+ FAQs and integrated with their existing systems, demonstrating GreenM's ability to deliver at scale.

*Why:* The correct version uses one anonymous deployment detail to make a point about a universal pattern. The incorrect version uses the client to showcase GreenM's capability. The subject of the post flips from the insight to the company.

---

### 3. Honest about complexity — not smoothing it over

**✓ Correct**
> Scaling AI in healthcare isn't a deployment problem. It's a coordination problem. The model generalises. The workflows don't. Each site has enough local variation that the operational layer needs to be partially rebuilt every time.

**✗ Incorrect**
> With the right partner, scaling AI across multiple sites is straightforward. GreenM's proven framework handles the complexity so your team doesn't have to.

*Why:* GreenM's credibility comes from naming what is actually hard. The correct version tells the reader something true and uncomfortable. The incorrect version uses "straightforward" and "handles the complexity" to position GreenM as a solution before the reader has agreed there's a problem.

---

### 4. Capability stated as operational fact, not as a claim

**✓ Correct**
> Private deployment in 2026 means zero patient data leaves the organisation's infrastructure. The AI runs inside the compliance boundary. HIPAA, GDPR, and NHS DSPT aren't afterthoughts bolted on during procurement. They're the foundation.

**✗ Incorrect**
> GreenM is proud to offer fully HIPAA, GDPR, and NHS DSPT compliant private deployment solutions, ensuring your patient data remains completely secure at all times.

*Why:* The correct version states what private deployment means in practice — no hedging, no "proud to offer", no "solutions". The capability is described as a condition of the work, not a feature being marketed.

---

### 5. CTA that invites peers, not leads

**✓ Correct**
> What questions does your compliance team ask before approving an AI deployment?

**✓ Also correct**
> What's been the biggest integration headache in your healthcare AI work?

**✗ Incorrect**
> Ready to see how GreenM can transform your AI deployment? Book a discovery call today and let's discuss your needs.

**✗ Also incorrect**
> Want to learn more? Download our guide to private AI deployment for healthcare organisations.

*Why:* GreenM's questions invite practitioners to share their experience — they treat the reader as a peer with relevant knowledge. The incorrect versions treat the reader as a prospect to be converted. The difference is in who the post is for: the reader's problem, or GreenM's pipeline.

## Marginal Calibration Examples — When the Voice Slips Without Breaking Rules

*This section is a working hypothesis. The failure modes below were derived from patterns visible in the voice rules themselves — places where a draft can pass every Tier 1 check (no forbidden words, no em dashes, blame redirected, insight-before-company, British or International English consistent) and still not sound like GreenM. They should be refined with real examples from edits as the team identifies them.*

The previous calibration examples contrast strong correct versions against obviously bad vendor-y versions. That gap is too wide to catch the most common drift, which happens between drafts that all formally pass the rules. The examples below show closer pairs — a near-miss that obeys every rule, against the version that lands.

---

### 6. Wrong distance from the work — observer language instead of practitioner language

**✓ Correct**
> In every healthcare AI project GreenM has delivered, the integration layer has been the most time-consuming, the most underestimated, and the most critical component.

**△ Marginal (passes all rules but voice slips)**
> Healthcare organisations should ensure the integration layer is properly scoped — it tends to be the most time-consuming and underestimated component of AI deployment projects.

*Why:* The marginal version has the same observation, no forbidden words, structurally fine. But the voice stands outside the work. "Organisations should ensure" places the writer in an advisory position, looking at the work from a distance. The correct version places the writer inside the projects — the authority comes from having delivered them, not from prescribing best practice. GreenM's voice loses credibility when it slips into advisory mode, because anyone can give advice about healthcare AI; the differentiation is in having shipped it.

---

### 7. Capability statement without operational anchor

**✓ Correct**
> Private deployment in 2026 means zero patient data leaves the organisation's infrastructure. The AI runs inside the compliance boundary.

**△ Marginal (passes all rules but voice slips)**
> GreenM specialises in private AI deployments that keep patient data secure and ensure compliance with healthcare regulations including HIPAA, GDPR, and NHS DSPT.

*Why:* The marginal version is honest, free of forbidden words (no "cutting-edge", no "industry-leading"), names the actual frameworks. But it reads as a capability list — what GreenM does, presented as marketing copy. The correct version describes the same capability as an operational condition: this is what "private deployment" actually means in practice. The reader learns something true about the work. Capability statements that don't teach the reader anything new about the underlying engineering decision feel like positioning, even when every word is technically correct.

---

### 8. Closing that lands as a summary, not as a peer question

**✓ Correct**
> What's been the biggest integration headache in your healthcare AI work?

**△ Marginal (passes all rules but voice slips)**
> These are the lessons we keep learning across deployments — what has your experience taught you about integration?

*Why:* The marginal version is technically a question. It invites the reader's experience. It has no CTA, no booking link, no vendor framing. But it does two things wrong before getting to the question. It recaps what was just said ("the lessons we keep learning") — which makes the post end with a self-referential beat instead of a clean handoff to the reader. And the framing "what has your experience taught you" is more abstract than the operational specificity GreenM's closers usually carry. The correct version asks about a concrete thing ("the biggest integration headache") that the reader can answer with a specific example, not a reflection.

## Self-Check Before Publishing Any GreenM Content

The checks are sorted by severity. Tier 1 failures block publishing — they are non-negotiable signals that the content is off-voice and will damage credibility. Tier 2 failures should be fixed if time permits — the content will work, but it falls short of the voice's best version. Tier 3 are calibration checks that matter for pattern-level quality across multiple pieces; they are worth reviewing after publication if not before.

### Tier 1 — Block Publishing

Run these on every piece. If any fails, do not publish until fixed.

1. **Forbidden words and phrases.** Run a check against the Tier 1 list above (vendor hype, AI signals, generic openers). Any match must be removed or rewritten, unless it appears as a proper noun.
2. **Em dashes.** Zero em dashes anywhere in the text. Search the draft and remove every one.
3. **Blame redirection.** Where the content describes failure or difficulty, confirm blame is redirected to structural causes (governance, ownership, design), not to individuals or client teams.
4. **CTA appropriateness.** No "Book a demo", no "Start your free trial", no language that treats the reader as a lead to be converted (service pages excepted, where direct CTAs are appropriate but still grounded — see Format Adaptation).
5. **Language variant consistency.** Confirm one variant throughout (British or International English, see Register section for decision rule). No mixing within a single piece. If British, consistent use of organisation, behaviour, recognise, programme, centre, licence (noun), labour. If International, avoid strong UK markers (whilst, amongst, programme as "schedule", lift, queue).
6. **Verifiable claims.** Any number, study, or factual claim must be either verifiable from GreenM's delivery experience, framed as observation ("from the deployments we've run"), cited from a verified source, or removed.

### Tier 2 — Fix If Time Permits

These are quality checks. The content can publish without them, but it will be weaker.

7. Does it read as written by people who have delivered these projects — or by someone who has read about the category?
8. Is there at least one specific operational detail that grounds the main point?
9. Is there at most one client reference, used to illustrate rather than to showcase?
10. Does it avoid the "capability list" structure (X does A, B, C, D)?
11. Are Tier 2 high-risk words (robust, navigate, foster, landscape, etc.) each grounded in a concrete operational detail in the same or following sentence?
12. Does the content treat the reader as a peer solving a real problem — not as a lead to be converted?

### Tier 3 — Calibration Across Multiple Pieces

These are pattern-level checks. Run them periodically (weekly or after every three pieces), not on every individual draft.

13. Do recent pieces share an opening move (industry problem, operational detail, counterintuitive statement, field observation, outcome)? If yes, the next piece must use a different one.
14. Does GreenM appear in the text at the same position each time (always in paragraph one, or always at the end)? Vary placement.
15. Do recent pieces share a closer type? Rotate between open question, unresolved tension, operational statement, invitation to share experience.
16. Does the emotional register match the weight of the subject — or has every piece been written at the same tempo regardless of topic?

## Voice Maintenance — Keeping This Guide Useful Over Time

A voice guide that never updates becomes a constraint instead of a tool. Markets shift, audiences change, what worked in 2025 stops working in 2027. But a voice guide that updates too eagerly loses its anchor — every weak draft can rationalise a rule change. The process below sets a deliberate cadence for review and a small set of triggers that justify pulling rules out of cycle.

*This is a generic template. Adapt the roles, cadences, and inputs to match GreenM's actual working reality.*

### Three Loops, Three Cadences

The guide is maintained through three loops with different speeds and different triggers.

**Loop 1 — Per-edit reactive (always-on).**
When a specific rule blocks a piece of content that should clearly work, flag the case immediately. Do not silently override the rule in the draft, and do not silently follow it at the cost of the piece. Both options corrupt the guide — the first by treating it as advisory, the second by treating it as inflexible. Instead: note the rule, the case where it failed, and the suggested fix in a running log (a single document, a Slack channel, a Linear issue queue — whatever holds friction lowest). The log is reviewed at Loop 2 cadence. This loop has no decision rights — it captures cases, it does not change rules.

**Loop 2 — Pattern review (monthly).**
Once a month, the person responsible for this guide reviews:
- The reactive log from Loop 1 (all flagged rule-failures from the past month)
- The Tier 3 calibration checks from the Self-Check across all content produced (opener variety, GreenM placement variety, closer variety, register-subject match)
- The Marginal Calibration Examples section against actual drafts edited — has the team caught any new failure modes that aren't yet documented?

For each item, decide one of three outcomes:
1. *Rule stands, edit the draft.* The rule is correct; the draft was wrong; no guide change.
2. *Rule needs clarification or scope refinement.* Add an exception, narrow the scope, or note a context where the rule doesn't apply (a common case for GreenM: a rule that works for thought leadership but breaks for service pages or sales material).
3. *Rule needs to change or be removed.* Rare. Requires evidence from multiple cases, not a single edit.

Changes from Loop 2 are made directly in the guide with a brief commit message or change note describing what changed and why.

**Loop 3 — Performance review (quarterly).**
Once a quarter, review the guide against external signals — not edits, but outcomes.

Inputs include any of the following that are available:
- Engagement metrics on LinkedIn company posts (impressions, comments, comment quality from target audiences)
- Blog traffic and engagement for content produced under this guide
- Linkbuilding placement performance and reception in host publications
- Conversion signals on service pages and sales materials where attribution is plausible
- Feedback from prospects, clients, partners on specific pieces
- Win/loss notes from sales conversations that reference specific content
- Anything the team has observed about what is working and what is being ignored

The question at Loop 3 is different: not "did this draft pass the rules", but "are these rules still pointing at what we want the voice to do?" If the diagnostic stance reliably gets more engagement than alternative framings, the rule is doing its job. If posts that bend a rule consistently outperform posts that follow it, the rule needs scrutiny.

Loop 3 can result in larger structural changes — adding a new section, deprecating one, reworking the Diagnostic-to-Solution matrix, adjusting which formats sit where on the commercial-intent spectrum. Changes from Loop 3 are made with a longer note in the guide explaining the data behind the decision, so the rationale stays visible.

### Decision Rights

For each loop, name:
- Who can **flag** an item (Loops 1, 2): anyone producing GreenM content
- Who can **review** and decide (Loops 2, 3): the named guide owner, typically the person closest to both the writing and the brand strategy
- Who must **approve** changes that affect brand voice character (not style mechanics): GreenM leadership — typically the CEO or whoever has final responsibility for how the brand sounds in market. Changes to core stance (insight-before-company, honesty about complexity, peer-to-peer relationship with reader), to the Diagnostic-to-Solution matrix, or to commercial-intent positioning require this approval. Style-mechanical changes (adding to forbidden lists, updating thresholds, clarifying scope, adding marginal examples) do not require this approval.

The split matters because it prevents the brand voice from drifting through small accumulations of decisions made by people who weren't part of defining the voice in the first place. Brand voice character is leadership's responsibility; the guide is just the place it gets written down.

### What to Avoid

The most common failure modes in voice-guide maintenance:

- **Loop 1 becoming silent.** People stop logging the friction because logging feels bureaucratic. The guide stops getting accurate inputs. Counter: keep the log radically lightweight. One sentence per case. No template required.
- **Loop 2 becoming defensive.** Reviews where every flagged case results in "rule stands, edit the draft" — even when the same case appears repeatedly. This is the guide hardening against legitimate signal. Counter: any case that has appeared three times in the log must result in option 2 or 3, not option 1.
- **Loop 3 over-reacting to single metrics.** One post outperforms by 3x, the team rewrites a rule. The variance was probably noise. Counter: structural changes require evidence from at least three independent cases, not one outlier.
- **Loop 3 over-reacting to commercial pressure.** A sales team requests changes that would soften the diagnostic stance because prospects ask for clearer pitches. This is the moment the guide is most at risk — commercial pressure to dilute the voice always sounds reasonable in the room. Counter: changes to commercial-format rules (service pages, sales materials) can be made through Loop 3. Changes to thought leadership and brand voice character cannot be justified by sales pipeline arguments alone — they require evidence that the audience the brand is built for has shifted.
- **Treating the guide as final.** Voice guides earn their authority through use, not through completeness. A guide that is missing sections (format coverage, calibration examples, feedback loops) but is honest about what it covers is more useful than a guide that pretends to cover everything.

The goal is not a perfect document. The goal is a document that stays useful as the writing gets done.
